What is the 70% rule?

Max allowable offer (MAO) = 70% × ARV − Rehab budget

The 70% rule is the house flipper's classic first-pass screen: pay no more than 70% of the after-repair value (ARV) of a property, minus what the renovation will cost. A house that should sell for $300,000 once renovated and needs $50,000 of work prices out at 70% × $300,000 − $50,000 = $160,000. Offer more than that and — the rule says — the deal's margin gets eaten by all the costs a flip quietly accumulates between closing and resale.

Where the other 30% actually goes

The rule looks arbitrary until you itemize what the 30% discount has to pay for. Take the default deal above — bought at exactly the 70% rule price, financed with a typical hard-money loan (85% of purchase + rehab at 10.5% interest-only and 2 points), held six months, sold with 8% selling costs:

30% of the $300,000 ARV$90,000
Selling costs — commission + seller closing (8% of resale)$24,000
Financing — 2 points + 6 months interest on $178,500$12,941
Purchase closing + 6 months holding costs$6,600
Left over: your pre-tax profit (15.5% of resale)$46,459

That is the whole logic of the rule: roughly half of the 30% goes to transaction and carry costs, and the other half is the profit that justifies the risk. The calculator computes each line for your actual numbers rather than trusting the shorthand — change the hold to twelve months or the selling costs to 10% and watch the margin thin out in real time.

Because the loan covers 85% of purchase + rehab, the cash actually invested is $31,500 of down payment plus the closing, holding and financing costs — about $51,041 — so the deal returns roughly 91% on cash in six months. Leverage is why flippers tolerate hard-money pricing; it is also why a missed ARV hurts so much, since the debt doesn't shrink when the resale price does. The break-even resale here is about $249,501 — a 17% cushion below the $300,000 estimate.

When 70% is the wrong number

Experienced investors treat the percentage as a dial. In competitive or high-priced metros, true 70% deals rarely trade, and the fixed costs of a flip are a smaller share of a $600,000 resale — many flippers underwrite at 75–85% and rely on accurate cost estimates instead of a fat rule-of-thumb cushion. In slow or low-priced markets, where days-on-market stretch and a $90,000 house still costs real money to sell, 65% is safer. The rule also assumes a sale exit: if your plan is to refinance and rent instead — the BRRRR strategy — the equivalent discipline is keeping all-in cost at or under the ~70–75% of ARV a refinance lender will advance, and the deal must also carry the new mortgage (check it with our DSCR loan calculator).

What flips actually earned in 2026

Real-world margins are thinner than the folklore. ATTOM's Q1 2026 U.S. Home Flipping Report — covering 64,348 flips, about 8% of all home sales — put the typical gross profit at $66,000, a 25.4% return on the purchase price, with the typical flip taking 165 days. That was the first margin improvement after seven straight quarters of decline, but still well below the 29.6% of a year earlier. And gross means gross: ATTOM's figure is simply resale price minus purchase price, before rehab, financing, holding and selling costs. Run those against a 25% gross margin and it's clear why disciplined buying — the thing the 70% rule enforces — separates profitable flippers from busy ones.

Financing the flip: what hard money costs in 2026

Most flips are financed with short-term hard-money or fix-and-flip loans sized on the project rather than your income. Typical 2026 terms: 9–12% interest for experienced borrowers, 1–3 origination points, terms of 6–18 months, and loan caps around 70–75% of ARV — lenders enforce their own version of the 70% rule. All-in annualized cost, with points and fees counted, commonly lands in the 15–25% range. The calculator models an interest-only loan on the full balance from day one, which is slightly conservative — many lenders fund rehab in draws, so you pay interest only on what's been drawn. Note what a longer hold does to the numbers: at 10.5% on $178,500, every extra month is roughly $1,560 of interest plus holding costs, which is how a slow permit or a stale listing quietly erases a third of the margin.

Getting ARV right — the number everything leans on

Every output on this page is downstream of the ARV estimate. Base it on closed sales of already-renovated comparables — similar size (within ~20% of square footage), bed/bath count, age, lot and school zone, sold within the last 3–6 months, as close as possible and ideally in the same subdivision. Adjust honestly for differences, and don't average in unrenovated sales or active listings (asking prices are hopes, not comps). The appraiser your buyer's lender sends will do exactly this exercise; if your ARV can't survive it, the resale price funds the discovery. Know your numbers at an ARV 5–10% below your estimate — the break-even tile above tells you how much room you actually have. Limits of the rule

The 70% rule is a screen, not an underwrite. It says nothing about rehab estimate quality — the budget you subtract is only as good as your scope of work and contractor bids; nothing about time — six months and twelve months pass the same rule but return very different annualized results; and nothing about your cost structure — an agent-investor who lists for free, or an all-cash buyer paying no points or interest, can profitably pay more than 70%. Use the rule to reject deals fast, then let the full arithmetic above — or a complete month-by-month model — make the offer decision.
