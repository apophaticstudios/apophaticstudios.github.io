What is the 50% rule?

The 50% rule is a screening shortcut for rental property: assume that about half of gross rent goes to operating expenses, before the mortgage. What's left is net operating income (NOI); subtract the loan payment and you have a rough cash-flow estimate — fast enough to run on a listing while you're still standing in the driveway.

Operating expenses ≈ Gross rent × 50%
Cash flow ≈ Gross rent × 50% − Mortgage P&I

The "50%" is meant to swallow everything a landlord pays to keep the unit rented and standing: vacancy and credit loss, property taxes, landlord insurance, repairs and maintenance, a reserve for big-ticket replacements (CapEx), property management, leasing and turnover costs, and any HOA dues or utilities the owner covers. It deliberately excludes the mortgage — that's a financing choice, not a property cost — and it excludes your income taxes and non-cash depreciation. Some versions of the rule leave out management or HOA; we use the all-in version, because a rule that leaves out costs you'll actually pay isn't screening anything.

The rule earned its place because it fixes the most common beginner mistake: budgeting only for taxes and insurance and pretending vacancy, repairs and a future roof won't happen. But it is a population average, not a property forecast. The calculator above therefore does two things at once — it applies the rule, and it lets you replace the rule with a line-item budget so you can see how far apart the two land for this deal.

Worked example: $250,000 house, $2,200 rent

The defaults above describe a $250,000 single-family rental bought with 20% down at 6.5% on a 30-year loan, renting for $2,200/month, with a fully loaded line-item budget: 8% vacancy (one month a year), $3,200 property tax, $1,400 landlord insurance, 8% repairs, 5% CapEx reserve, 10% management on collected rent, and $700/year of leasing and turnover costs. Two things stand out. First, for a plain single-family rental with professional management, the line-item budget lands at 50.3% of rent — almost exactly what the rule predicts, which is why the rule has survived for decades. Second, passing the expense test doesn't mean the deal works: at 2026 rates and 20% down, this house loses about $165–170 a month once the mortgage is paid. The rule tells you it takes a price near $217,500 (the break-even-price tile) or rent of about $2,528 (the break-even-rent tile) for this deal to stop costing you money. That is the rule's real job — turning a listing into a target price in one step.

Repairs & maintenance5–10% of rentFour rules of thumb, all rough: 1% of property value per year; $1 per square foot per year; the 5x rule (annual repairs ≈ 1.5 × one month's rent); or 5–10% of rent. Older homes, older systems and cheap finishes push toward the top. Landscaping, snow and pest control belong here if you pay them.
CapEx reserve5–10% of rentMoney set aside for items that fail every 10–30 years: roof, HVAC, water heater, appliances, flooring, driveway, windows, plumbing. The precise method is replacement cost ÷ remaining life for each item, summed per month — e.g. an item you expect to replace for $12,000 in 15 years needs $12,000 ÷ 180 ≈ $67/month. Percent-of-rent is the shortcut when you don't yet know the property.
Property management8–12% of collected rent10% is the 2026 benchmark for single-family (DoorLoop); flat fees exist in some metros. Budget it even if you self-manage — your time isn't free, and the number should survive you hiring help. Watch for maintenance markups (10–15%) and setup fees.
Leasing & turnover$500–$1,500/yrTenant-placement fees run 50–100% of one month's rent; renewals $200–$500. Add make-ready costs (paint, cleaning, small repairs) each turn. Divide the per-turn total by expected years of tenancy — a $2,000 turn every three years is about $670/yr.
HOA dues$0–$500+/moFixed and non-negotiable for condos and many townhomes. Also budget for special assessments, and read the rental cap and reserve study before buying.
Landlord-paid utilities$0–$300+/moSingle-family tenants usually pay everything. In small multifamily the owner often pays water, sewer, trash, common-area electric and sometimes heat. Ask for 12 months of actual bills.
Other operating costs1–3% of rentBookkeeping software, rental licences and inspections (city-specific), advertising, legal and eviction costs ($300–$1,000+ per case when handled by a manager), accountant, mileage. Small individually; not zero in aggregate.

Not operating expenses: the mortgage principal and interest (debt service), income taxes, depreciation (a non-cash deduction), closing costs (part of your cash invested), and capital improvements (paid out of the CapEx reserve you already budgeted). Keep them out of the 50% and out of NOI, or your cap rate comparisons will be meaningless.

When the 50% rule is the wrong number

The rule works because, across many ordinary properties, percentage-based costs (vacancy, repairs, management) and dollar-based costs (taxes, insurance, turnover) happen to sum to about half of rent. Break either half of that coincidence and the rule fails — usually in the direction that flatters the deal.

Low-rent units

Taxes, insurance and a turnover don't shrink because rent is $950 instead of $2,200. A $1,200 insurance policy and $1,500 tax bill are 24% of a $950 rent before a single repair, and a $1,500 make-ready is 13% of a year's gross. Cheap properties routinely run 60–70% expense ratios, which is why many "cash cows" on paper barely cover themselves.

High-property-tax states

At Illinois's 1.84% average effective rate, taxes on a $250,000 house are about $4,600/yr — $383/month, or 17% of a $2,200 rent by themselves. Swap that into the default budget and the expense ratio jumps from 50% to about 56%. New Jersey (1.58%), Vermont (1.40%), Connecticut (1.36%) and Ohio (1.32%) create the same problem; Hawaii, Idaho, Wyoming, Arizona and Alabama (0.33–0.43%) go the other way.

Condos, HOAs and landlord-paid utilities

A $350 HOA fee is 16% of a $2,200 rent — on top of a budget that was already at 50%. Multifamily where the owner pays water, sewer and trash adds another 5–10%. Neither is optional, so the rule understates costs every time.

Older properties

A 1950s house with original plumbing, a 20-year-old roof and a furnace on borrowed time needs a CapEx reserve two or three times the 5% default. The rule doesn't know the age of the building; your inspection report does.

When 50% is too pessimistic

A newer, self-managed house in a low-tax state can run 35–40% with everything counted. Even then, keep the management line in your model: a deal that only works because you'll do the work for free is a job, not an investment.

50% rule vs. 1% rule vs. cap rate

These three screens answer different questions and are strongest together. The 1% rule compares rent to price (monthly rent ≥ 1% of purchase price). The 50% rule compares expenses to rent. Combine them and you get a cap rate: a property renting for exactly 1% of its price with a 50% expense ratio produces NOI of 0.5% of price per month — 6% per year — before financing. That is why "passes the 1% rule" was a decent proxy for "cash flows" when mortgage rates were 4%, and a poor one at 6.5–7%: the 6% unleveraged yield is now below the cost of debt, so leverage subtracts from cash flow instead of adding to it. Our default deal shows exactly that — rent is 0.88% of price, the expense ratio is 50%, and the loan costs more than the property nets.

Using the rule backwards: break-even price and rent

The most useful thing about a rule with one variable is that it solves in both directions. If cash flow must be at least zero, then the largest mortgage payment you can afford is rent × (1 − expense ratio), and the price follows from your loan terms:

Max P&I = Rent × (1 − ratio)
Break-even price = Max P&I ÷ payment factor ÷ (1 − down payment %)
Break-even rent = P&I ÷ (1 − ratio)

The payment factor is the standard mortgage constant for your rate and term — for 6.5% over 30 years it is about 0.00632 per dollar of loan, so $1,100 of available P&I supports a $174,000 loan and, with 20% down, a price of about $217,500. The calculator shows both break-even figures under the rule and under your itemized budget; the itemized version is the one to take into a negotiation, since it reflects the taxes and insurance actually attached to this house. Raising the down payment moves the break-even price up (less debt to service) but lowers cash-on-cash return — see the ROI calculator for that trade-off, or the DSCR loan calculator for how a lender would view the same numbers.

After you buy: replace the rule with the ledger

The 50% rule is a pre-purchase tool. Once you own the property, every line in the table above becomes an actual number in a bank statement, and the right expense ratio is whatever twelve months of receipts say it is. Track income and expenses by category from day one — for taxes (Schedule E wants them that way anyway), for refinancing (lenders ask for actuals), and so that your next deal's budget is built from your own history instead of a rule of thumb. If you're buying distressed and refinancing, the BRRRR calculator applies the same expense logic to the post-rehab rent; if you'd rather flip than hold, the 70% rule calculator is the equivalent one-variable screen for resale deals; and if the property might work as a short-term rental, the Airbnb calculator shows how different the expense stack becomes.
