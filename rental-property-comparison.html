How to compare rental properties

Analyzing one deal tells you whether it clears your bar. Comparing several tells you which one actually deserves your down payment — and that is the decision you are really making, because the cash can only be spent once. A useful comparison has four rules:

1. Same assumptions everywhere. Use one vacancy allowance, one maintenance-and-management percentage, and each property's actual tax and insurance bills. If Deal A gets 5% vacancy and Deal B gets 8% because you like Deal A, the spreadsheet is just decorating a decision you already made.

2. Same financing, unless it's genuinely different. Hold down payment, rate and term constant so the buildings compete, not the loans. Then vary financing on the leader alone. The exception: when properties really are quoted different terms — condos and 2–4-unit buildings often price a notch higher — model what you were actually quoted, as the defaults below do for the duplex.

3. Rank on more than one number. The deal that makes the most dollars per month is frequently not the deal that earns the most per dollar invested. Look at both, plus the property-only metrics (cap rate, GRM) that ignore your loan.

4. Net, never gross. Comparing rents or prices alone is how condos with $220 HOA fees and houses in 1.8%-property-tax counties sneak to the top of the list. Every metric in the table above is computed after vacancy and operating expenses.

The six numbers that decide it

Cap rate = NOI ÷ price  ·  Cash-on-cash = annual cash flow ÷ cash invested
DSCR = NOI ÷ mortgage payment  ·  GRM = price ÷ annual gross rent

Cap rate compares the properties themselves — what each earns per dollar of price before any mortgage. Cash-on-cash return compares your outcomes — what each deal pays per dollar of your cash after the mortgage; for most leveraged buyers it is the ranking metric. Monthly cash flow is the margin of safety in dollars: a deal can post a fine percentage on a tiny base and still leave no room for a surprise repair. DSCR is how a lender will rank the same list — NOI over debt service, with 1.20+ the common bar. The 1% rule and GRM are price-to-rent screens (they ignore expenses entirely): rent ÷ price above 1% and a lower GRM both say you're buying rent cheaply — useful for a first pass, never for a verdict, which is why the 50% rule exists to check the expense side.

That is the argument for comparing: the house looks acceptable alone and loses to both alternatives side by side.

Which of the two winners should get the down payment? Notice the condo uses $36,300 of cash to the duplex's $86,400. An investor with about $90,000 could buy the duplex — or buy the condo and still have $50,000 toward the next deal. Comparing "one duplex" against "condo plus the option on a second property" is the real choice, and it is why cash-on-cash, not monthly dollars, is usually the tiebreaker for investors who plan to keep buying.

When cap rate and cash-on-cash disagree

With no loan, cash-on-cash roughly equals the cap rate (closing costs make it slightly lower). A mortgage moves the two apart, and the direction depends on how the loan's cost compares with the property's yield: financing at a rate below the cap rate levers returns up; financing above it levers them down. At 2026 rates that second case is the usual one — 30-year investment-property loans were quoting roughly 7.38–7.88%, about 0.5–1.0 point above primary-residence rates, as of September 4, 2026 (The Mortgage Reports) — which is why every deal in the example shows cash-on-cash below its cap rate. The defaults use 6.5–6.75% as a round, well-qualified-borrower scenario; type in the rate you were actually quoted and watch what happens. At 7.5% across the board, the house flips to roughly −$88 a month, the duplex's cash flow drops to about $116, and the condo's cash-on-cash falls from 4.24% to about 1.3% — same properties, different ranking pressure, purely from financing. When rates are above cap rates, more leverage means less return, and the deals that survive are the ones that were priced right, not financed cleverly. (Buying below market and forcing the rent up is the other escape — that's the BRRRR play.)

Mistakes that flip a ranking

Different assumptions per deal. The subtle version: copying the listing agent's "estimated taxes" for one property and the county's actual bill for another. Pull the real tax bill and an insurance quote for each — across states, taxes alone range from about 0.3% to 1.8% of value per year, easily the difference between first and last place.

Comparing gross instead of net. The condo above would look worse than the house on price-per-rent if you skipped the HOA fee — and better than it deserves if you skipped everyone's operating expenses. Net operating income is the only fair meeting point.

Ignoring unequal cash. A deal that "wins" while consuming twice the capital hasn't won per dollar. Compare cash-on-cash, and think about what the leftover cash could buy.

One-number shopping. Ranking purely on cap rate hands the win to rough neighborhoods and deferred maintenance, which price at high caps for a reason; ranking purely on the 1% rule does the same. High-yield-on-paper is often just risk that hasn't happened yet, so treat an outlier metric as a question, not an answer.

Stopping at year one. Everything on this page is a snapshot. Rent growth, appreciation, loan paydown and eventual sale can re-rank deals over a 5-year horizon — a market with 6% rent growth beats a point of first-year yield surprisingly fast. That's a projection job, not a screening job; it's what the full spreadsheet is for.

How many deals should you compare?

More than you'd think. Experienced buy-and-hold investors talk about funnels, not finds: screen dozens of listings with the fast filters (1% rule, GRM, asking-price cap rate), run the full numbers on the handful that pass, offer on a few, and buy the one where the numbers and the inspection both hold. A side-by-side table is what keeps the funnel honest — deals compete against each other instead of against your patience. Screen fast with this page; when a deal makes the short list, underwrite it properly: line-item expenses (the full list), lender-style DSCR, and — if it's a project — the BRRRR or flip math, or the short-term-rental alternative.
