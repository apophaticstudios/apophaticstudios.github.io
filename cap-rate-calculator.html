How to calculate cap rate

The capitalization rate ("cap rate") measures a rental property's unleveraged annual yield — what the property earns relative to its price, ignoring any mortgage. The formula has two ingredients:

Cap rate = Net operating income ÷ Purchase price × 100

Net operating income (NOI) is your annual rental income after a vacancy allowance and all operating expenses — property taxes, insurance, maintenance and repairs, property management, and any HOA or utilities you pay — but before the mortgage payment and before capital expenditures like a roof replacement. Because financing is excluded, two investors with completely different loans can use cap rate to compare the same property on equal terms.

A worked example

Take a $250,000 property renting for $2,200/month with 5% vacancy, $3,200/year in property taxes, $1,400 insurance, $2,400 maintenance, and 8% property management:

Gross rent (12 × $2,200)$26,400
Less vacancy (5%)−$1,320
Effective gross income$25,080
Less property tax−$3,200
Less insurance−$1,400
Less maintenance & repairs−$2,400
Less management (8% of gross rent)−$2,112
Net operating income$15,968

Cap rate = $15,968 ÷ $250,000 = 6.39%. That's the default scenario loaded in the calculator above — change any input and every figure updates.

What is a good cap rate?

There is no single "good" number, because cap rate prices risk. A pristine property in a strong, expensive market might trade at a 4–5% cap; a rougher property in a declining area might need 10%+ to attract buyers. Many residential buy-and-hold investors screen for roughly 5–8%, then dig deeper. Treat an unusually high cap rate as a question — why is this property so cheap relative to its income? — rather than an automatic win. Local knowledge beats any rule of thumb: compare against recent sales of similar rentals in the same submarket.

Cap rate vs. cash-on-cash return

Cap rate ignores your loan; cash-on-cash return embraces it. Cash-on-cash divides your annual cash flow after the mortgage payment by the actual cash you invested (down payment plus closing costs), so it tells you what your money is earning once leverage is in play. A deal can have a modest cap rate and a strong cash-on-cash return with the right financing — or the reverse. Use cap rate to compare properties, cash-on-cash to compare uses of your capital, and our free ROI calculator to see both side by side with DSCR and the 1% rule.

Using cap rate to set your offer price

The formula also runs backwards: Price = NOI ÷ target cap rate. If a property produces $15,968 of NOI and you require a 6% cap, the most you can pay is $15,968 ÷ 0.06 ≈ $266,000. The "price at target cap" tile above does this automatically — it's a fast way to turn a listing's numbers into a maximum offer.

Limitations to keep in mind

Cap rate is a snapshot, not a forecast. It says nothing about appreciation, rent growth, taxes on sale, or the capital expenditures an older property will demand. It's also only as honest as the expense numbers you feed it — listings often quote "pro forma" NOI with optimistic vacancy and skinny maintenance. Rebuild NOI from your own assumptions before trusting any advertised cap rate.
