What is the 1% rule?

The 1% rule is the fastest screen in rental real estate: a property is worth a closer look if the monthly gross rent is at least 1% of the purchase price (in the careful version, purchase price plus upfront repairs).

Rent-to-price ratio = Monthly rent ÷ Purchase price × 100  →  pass at ≥ 1%

A $250,000 house must rent for about $2,500/month to pass; a $2,200 rent passes only up to a $220,000 price. The rule uses gross rent — before vacancy, expenses or the mortgage — which is exactly why it can only ever be a filter. It answers "should I bother running the numbers?", not "will this make money?". For the second question you need a real cash-flow analysis.

Why 1% used to mean cash flow — and why it doesn't anymore

Pair the 1% rule with the 50% rule and a passing deal generates NOI of about 6% of the purchase price per year (12 × 1% × 50%). Whether that cash-flows depends entirely on what the debt costs — and that's what changed. The annual cost of a 30-year loan (the "mortgage constant") was about 5.7% of the balance at 4% rates; at 7.5% it's 8.4%. Same rule, same property, opposite result:

At the 4–5% rates of the 2010s, a deal at 0.8–0.9% could still cash flow — the 1% rule had margin built in. As of September 8, 2026, 30-year investment-property rates run about 7.40–7.90% (roughly 0.5–1 point above the ~6.9% primary-residence rate, quoted for a strong borrower with 40% down, per The Mortgage Reports). At those rates the honest version of the 1% rule is closer to a 1.1–1.2% rule at 20% down. Bigger down payments lower the bar — and an all-cash buyer only needs rent to clear expenses. Lenders run a version of the same test as DSCR; a deal near its break-even ratio will also sit near the 1.0–1.2 DSCR floor most lenders want.

What the typical property actually rents for in 2026

Nationally, the rule fails before you even reach the mortgage. The median existing home sold for $434,100 in July 2026 (up 2.0% year over year, the 37th straight month of increases, per NAR), while the typical US asking rent was $1,962 (Zillow Observed Rent Index, up 2.3% year over year). That's a rent-to-price ratio of about 0.45% — under half the rule — and even using the higher single-family rent of $2,314 it's only 0.53%. This is why 1% deals are hunted, not browsed: they live where prices are low relative to rents, not in the typical listing. Regional medians tell you where to look — the Midwest's $342,900 median price versus $622,200 in the West is most of the story.

Where deals still pass

BiggerPockets' summer 2026 rent-to-payment study (54 metros, July 2026) compared average market rent against the full monthly payment — principal, interest, taxes and insurance — and found the average metro covered only about 80% of the payment. The Midwest was the only region at roughly full coverage (1.01), led by Detroit at 1.99, where the average home value near $72,000 against ~$1,280 rent works out to about a 1.8% monthly rent-to-price ratio; Cleveland, St. Louis, Cincinnati, Indianapolis and Columbus clustered next. At the other end, San Jose (0.39), Austin (0.40), Los Angeles and Seattle (0.49) don't come close. Two cautions before you book a flight: low-priced properties carry proportionally heavier repair, turnover and CapEx loads — check every line against the rental expense list — and metro averages hide block-by-block variation that only local knowledge catches. The other route to a passing ratio is to manufacture it: buy under market, renovate, and let the BRRRR math reset your basis.

The 2% rule (and where it came from)

The 2% rule is the same screen with a stricter bar — and with 50% expenses it implies roughly a 12% cap rate, double the 1% rule's 6% (see how the rules chain together on our cap rate page). It dates from the years after 2008, when foreclosure-heavy markets sold houses for less than the cost to build them while rents barely moved. Today 2% appears almost exclusively in very low-priced properties, because rents have a floor and prices don't: a $60,000 house renting for $1,100 hits 1.8% without being a bargain. Treat a very high ratio as information, not free money — it usually prices in condition, tenant turnover, collections risk and management effort that a percentage can't see.

1% rule vs. 50% rule vs. cap rate

The three classic screens check different sides of the same deal. The 1% rule tests whether the income is big enough relative to the price. The 50% rule tests whether you're being honest about operating costs. Cap rate combines both into an unleveraged yield — and passing 1% with 50% expenses is arithmetically the same as a 6% cap. None of them see your loan, which in 2026 is where deals die; that's what the break-even tile above adds. When a deal clears the screens, run it through the free ROI calculator for cash flow, cash-on-cash and DSCR — and if you're choosing among several, put them side by side in the deal comparison calculator.

What the rule misses

Everything except price and rent. Property taxes that range from 0.33% (Hawaii) to 1.84% (Illinois) of value per year, insurance that differs by multiples between states, HOA dues, condition and deferred CapEx, appreciation and rent growth, your financing — none of it is in the ratio. Two properties at exactly 1.0% can be a keeper and a money pit. Use the rule for what it's good at — killing obvious non-starters in seconds while you're scanning listings — and underwrite the survivors properly.
