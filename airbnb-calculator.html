How to estimate Airbnb revenue

Short-term rental revenue has two parts: nightly revenue and cleaning-fee income. Nightly revenue is your average daily rate times the nights you actually fill; cleaning income is the fee you charge per stay times the number of stays (occupied nights ÷ average length of stay).

Gross revenue = ADR × (365 × occupancy) + cleaning fee × stays

The most common underwriting mistake is multiplying the listing price by 365. Empty nights earn nothing — which is why operators track RevPAR (revenue per available night = ADR × occupancy). A $230 listing at 62% occupancy is really a $143/night property. The second most common mistake is stopping at revenue: platform fees, turnovers, utilities and furnishing consume a large share of it, which is what the rest of this calculator measures.

That's not a bearish thumb on the scale; it's what full-price deals at 2026 rates look like. STR returns are made by buying below market, raising RevPAR (better listing, dynamic pricing, longer season), or borrowing cheaper — and the calculator lets you test each lever.

What Airbnb actually takes in 2026

Airbnb is migrating every host to a host-only service fee of 15.5%, replacing the old split structure (host paid ~3%, guest paid a separate service fee). The fee applies to the nightly rate plus every host-set charge — cleaning fees, pet fees, extra-guest fees — though not taxes. US and Canadian software-connected hosts switched on October 27, 2025; the remaining software-connected hosts followed April 13, 2026; independent hosts outside the EU move by September 15, 2026, and EU hosts by October 13, 2026. Reservations confirmed before your migration date keep their old terms.

For comparison, Vrbo charges pay-per-booking hosts a 5% commission plus a 3% payment-processing fee (~8% total), and direct bookings cost only your payment processing. The channel dropdown above prices each one — on the default deal, switching the same bookings from a 15.5% fee to a direct-booking site changes cash flow by over $9,700 a year, which is why established operators work so hard on repeat and direct business.

The 2026 short-term rental market in numbers

Per AirDNA's 2026 US outlook: forecast occupancy of 57.4% (just above the pre-pandemic average of 57.0%), RevPAR growth of about 2.9%, and demand and new supply each growing roughly 2.7% — slowing supply growth is finally restoring pricing power after the post-2021 listing boom. Nightly-rate growth accelerated from 0.7% year-over-year in January to about 3% by spring. Two cautions: national averages hide huge market-level spread (top metros posted 10%+ RevPAR growth while others declined), and international inbound demand has been soft. Underwrite with market-level comps for your street and property type, not the national number — then stress-test occupancy 10–20% below your estimate.

The expenses first-time hosts forget

Industry benchmarks put total STR operating costs at roughly 30–70% of revenue — far above a long-term rental's ratio. The line items that most often go missing from a first analysis: the cleaning you pay for (your $120 turnover cost is charged every stay whether or not the $140 guest fee covers it), utilities and internet (you pay them, not a tenant), supplies and consumables (linens, toiletries, coffee — plus pricing software and a channel manager), STR-specific insurance (a landlord policy typically won't cover commercial nightly use), professional management if you're not self-managing (commonly 20–30% of revenue, versus 8–10% for long-term rentals), and the furnishing and startup budget — easily $20,000–40,000 for a whole home, and it belongs in your cash-invested base, where it quietly drags down cash-on-cash return.

Break-even occupancy: the number that keeps you safe

Some of your costs scale with bookings (platform fees, cleaning, management, maintenance reserves) — but the mortgage, taxes, insurance, utilities and HOA arrive whether anyone books or not. Break-even occupancy is the occupancy at which contribution from bookings exactly covers those fixed costs. The calculator solves it in closed form and shows your cushion — the gap between planned and break-even occupancy. A deal that breaks even at 45% when the market runs 60% can absorb a bad season or a new competitor next door; a deal that needs 60% in a 57% market is priced for perfection.

What this calculator doesn't model

This is an annualized screen, not a full pro forma. It holds ADR and occupancy flat across the year, while real STR markets are strongly seasonal — a beach town might do 90% July occupancy and 25% in January, which changes monthly cash needs even when the annual average matches. It also excludes lodging/occupancy taxes (usually collected on top of your rates and remitted, but check your jurisdiction), permits and licensing fees, HOA or municipal STR restrictions — verify these before buying, since a ban zeroes the model — and income-tax effects.
